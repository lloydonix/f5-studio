First, the contract must be in writing.
Second, it must be signed by both parties.
Third, it must be executed before the celebration of the marriage.

The trap: the writing requirement is for validity (Art. 77), not for enforceability against third persons (that is Art. 1403). Do not confuse the two. If you do, the professor will mark you wrong.

Now, Art. 76 tells you when a modification is allowed. Any change to the settlement must also be made before the wedding. After the vows, the property regime is locked; you cannot rewrite it. Memorize that; it’s the same rule as the original settlement, just applied to amendments.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
ARTICLES 81 AND 92 – VOID IF NO MARRIAGE, EXCLUSIONS FROM COMMUNITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Listen. Art. 81 is simple: If the marriage does not occur, everything stipulated in the settlement collapses. Anything that depended on the marriage—donations, promises, conditions—becomes void. Do not add any “but” clause; the professor warned you not to assume facts. Recite: “No wedding, no settlement.” Again. Good.

Art. 92 lists three exclusions from the absolute community of property. Count them for me, one line each.

1. Property acquired by gratuitous title during the marriage, and its fruits, unless expressly included by the donor.
2. Property for personal and exclusive use of either spouse, except jewelry, which does become community property.
3. Property acquired before the marriage by a spouse who has legitimate descendants from a former marriage, and its fruits.

The exam loves to test the jewelry exception. Remember: jewelry is community even if it is for personal use. Say “jewelry joins” and smile.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
ARTICLE 96 – JOINT ADMINISTRATION AND HUSBAND’S PREEMINENCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Now the administration rule. Both spouses jointly administer community property. If they disagree, the husband’s decision prevails. The wife may seek court relief within five years from the date of the contested act. Say “husband decides, wife courts, five years.” Again. Hm. Good.

A disposition or encumbrance without the other spouse’s written consent or court authority is void. Yet the law calls it a continuing offer: the other spouse may accept before the offer is withdrawn. Do not muddle “void” with “voidable.” It is void ab initio unless the offer is accepted in time. Recite: “void without consent, offer remains open.” Good.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
ARTICLE 43, PARAGRAPH 2 – COMMON PROVISION FOR ALL REGIMES
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Article 43, paragraph 2 is the same across all property regimes. It states that the administration of the conjugal partnership or absolute community belongs to both spouses jointly. The rule you just heard for Art. 96 mirrors this. No extra nuance here; just remember it applies universally. Say “joint admin, all regimes.” Good.

CASE LAW – KEY DOCTRINES
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The professor flagged three cases. I will drill them; you will repeat the holding.

ACP v. CPG – The issue was whether the property in dispute belonged to the absolute community of property or to the conjugal partnership. The Court held that the nature of the regime determines ownership, and the burden of proof lies on the party asserting community ownership. Say “regime decides ownership.” Again. Good.

Josef v. Ursua – Emerging doctrine on Art. 148, not Art. 147. The Court recognized that a void marriage (void ab initio) renders any subsequent property regime inexistent, affecting the classification of property. Memorize: “void marriage, no regime.” Say it. Good.

Cua-Ko v. Republic – The right to legitimate parentage belongs to the child, not the biological father, when the child is born out of wedlock and the mother is the legitimate parent. The Court emphasized the best interests of the child. Recite: “child’s right, not father’s.” Good.

Lucas v. Lucas – The Court warned against unfettered DNA claims; it held that paternity can be contested only under specific statutory grounds, preventing frivolous claims. Say “DNA claim limited.” Good.

Sit. Listen. The next part of your exam is the termination of marriage and its effects on property. I will not waste time with fluff. I will not even let you think you can skip it. You will remember it because you will have to say it in your sleep. That is the only way to survive the bar.

First, the Family Code gives the effects of a subsequent marriage. Art. 43, paragraph (2) is the one you must memorize. The guilty spouse loses his share of the net profits of the community or conjugal partnership. The forfeiture order is: common children first, then the guilty spouse’s children from a previous marriage, and only then the innocent spouse. No one can touch what the innocent spouse already owns. Remember that. If you forget, you will be caught in the trap of thinking the forfeiture goes to the innocent spouse first. That is a mistake.

The termination of marriage is not the same as annulment or nullity. The law treats it as a *separate* event. The spouse who contracted the subsequent marriage in bad faith is disqualified to inherit from the innocent spouse by testate and intestate succession. The innocent spouse may revoke any designation of the bad-faith spouse as beneficiary in any insurance policy, even if the designation is irrevocable. This is a dead-penny rule. Forget it, and you will lose points for missing the nuance.

Now the property regimes. The line that separates absolute community property (ACP) from conjugal partnership of gains (CPG) is whether the property was owned before the marriage. ACP covers all property owned before the marriage; CPG covers only the fruits of that property. You will be asked to identify whether a property belongs to ACP or CPG. The default is ACP for marriages on or after August 3, 1988, and CPG for marriages before that date. The trap is that you may think that a property acquired during the marriage is always community. It is not; it depends on whether it was acquired by gratuitous title, by sale, by inheritance, etc. The mnemonic: "ACP for all before, CPG for the fruits only."

Josef v. Ursua is a landmark case for same-sex cohabitation. The Supreme Court held that Art. 148, not Art. 147, governs the property of same-sex partners who live together. Art. 147 covers a man and a woman capacitated to marry each other. Art. 148 covers every other cohabitation, where ownership follows actual contribution. The court did not recognise same-sex marriage; therefore, Art. 148 applies. The partners acquire property in proportion to their contributions. Remember that. The case is recent, February 5, 2025, G.R. No. 267469. It is an emerging doctrine; you will be asked to identify the correct article.

Impugning legitimacy is another trap. The law says the husband *may* let it go. The word *may* is the key. You cannot assume the husband must do it. The court will look at whether he actually did it. The same applies to the right of the child. Cua Ko v. Republic holds that the right belongs to the child, not the biological father. You will be asked to apply that.

Who may adopt is a big one. Section 21 of the Family Code lists who may adopt. The most important thing is that it is the parent who may adopt, not the spouse. The law is clear: the adoptive parent must be a natural person, not a corporation. The test is whether the person is capable of caring for the child. Remember that.

Marriage is the foundation. The general principles are that it is a contract, it requires consent, it is solemnized, and it has legal effects. The law is not a game; it is a contract that you must respect. The professor warned you not to assume facts. You will be given a scenario and you must apply the law. Do not assume that all jewelry is part of absolute community property. That is a trap. The gratuity rule says that personal use, pre-marriage children, and jewelry are excluded; jewelry joins. Remember that.

Sit. Your notes are right—*may* is the word that will trip you. The husband *may* impugn, or he may not. That’s the difference between a child’s inheritance and a lawyer’s headache. Say it with me: *"May."* Again. Good.

The grounds are three: physical impossibility, biological proof, or defective artificial insemination. The first is the one they’ll test. *"First 120 days of the 300 days"*—count them backward from birth. No intercourse? Prove it. Separation? Show the lease, the divorce decree, the hospital records. Biological proof? DNA won’t save you if it’s not in your notes. Defective authorization? Only if the clinic didn’t get the husband’s signature. *"Defective"* means *missing*, not *forged*.

The periods are brutal. One year if you’re local, two if you’re provincial, three if you’re abroad. Start counting from the *earlier* of birth or registration. Most births get registered fast—so count from the day the child’s name hits the civil registry. Miss the deadline? The child inherits. Full stop.

The trap: the biological father *cannot* impugn. *[Cua Ko]* says the right belongs to the child. Not the sperm donor. Not the man who paid for the DNA test. The child. So if a client comes in with a birth certificate naming his ex-husband and demands a name change, remind him: the child gets to decide. At 18 (check: not in your notes). Or sooner, if the court lets him.

Now, the heirs’ exception. If the husband dies before the period expires, his heirs step in. If he dies *after* filing but before dropping the case, they keep going. If the child is born after his death? Too late. The clock stopped when he died.

Art. 84: If the regime is not absolute community, the future spouses may donate to each other in the settlement no more than one-fifth of their present property. Any excess is void. If the regime is ACP, the donation is useless because the spouses are already co-owners. Count them for me: one-fifth. One-fifth of present property.

Donations by reason of marriage: Art. 84 again. But Art. 87 says every donation or grant between spouses during the marriage is void, except moderate gifts on a family rejoicing. What is moderate? Art. 98: Neither spouse may donate community property to third persons without the other’s consent, except moderate donations for charity or family rejoicing or distress.

Art. 74: Property relations between spouses are governed by the marriage settlement, then the Family Code, then local customs. From “I do” the law treats them as one. Art. 81: If the marriage does not take place, everything stipulated in consideration of it is void. That’s the trap: you can’t create a prenup after marriage.

II. ABSOLUTE COMMUNITY OF PROPERTY (ARTS. 88–104) (CONTINUED, 1 OF 4)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Sit. Listen. This part covers the exclusions, the administration versus disposition line, and the rule on sales without consent. No moving until I say so.

Exclusions – Art. 92, FC. Three categories, count them for me.

1. Property acquired by gratuitous title during the marriage, and its fruits and income, unless the donor expressly says otherwise.
2. Property for personal and exclusive use of either spouse – jewelry is the lone exception, it joins the community.
3. Property owned before the marriage by a spouse who has legitimate descendants from a former marriage, and its fruits and income.

The trap: you look at the value only after you’ve decided the acquisition is onerous. If it’s gratuitous, the analysis stops. Say “excludes” for me. Again. Good.

Administration vs. Disposition – Art. 96, FC. Five-year rule for the wife’s administration of community property. Anything beyond administration is a disposition and needs both spouses’ consent.

- Lease, collection of rents, and ordinary repairs = administration.
- Sale, mortgage, or any encumbrance = disposition.

If the husband sells without consent, the act is void; the buyer gets nothing but the price, which must be returned to the community. The law calls it a “continuing offer” that never became valid.

Sale of Community Property Without Consent – The professor marked this herself. Ignoring it is not an option. The sale is void ab initio. A special power of attorney (SPA) does not cure the defect; the SPA is valid only if it itself complies with the consent requirement. The sale is a voidable act only if the buyer is a good-faith purchaser for value, but the community can still annul and recover the price. Remember: “void but a continuing offer” – that phrase belongs to the codal text.

Forfeiture of Net Profits – When the community property yields net profits, the order of distribution is:

1. Children born during the marriage (common children).
2. The guilty spouse’s children from a previous marriage.
3. The innocent spouse’s children from a previous marriage.

The trap: do not assume the guilty spouse’s children are always last. The law protects the children of the marriage first.

Abandonment and Separation in Fact – Three months of factual separation triggers sole administration for the spouse who remains. It also opens the door to judicial separation and receivership of the community assets.

Liquidation of the Absolute Community – Upon dissolution, you must:

- Take inventory of all community assets.
- Subtract exclusive (separate) property and debts.
- Split the remainder 50-50.
- Respect legitimes and the family dwelling (the dwelling stays with the spouse who has custody of the children, unless otherwise agreed).

Maternal Preference Rule – Art. 102, FC. If a child is under seven, custody goes to the mother unless she is unfit. The rule is a presumption, not an absolute bar.

Mandatory Separation on Remarriage – The professor teaches one year; Art. 103, FC actually says six months. On the exam, write the six-month period and note the professor’s “one-year” as a common trap.

Art. 130, FC – Mirrors the one-year rule for the conjugal partnership of gains. If you confuse it with the ACP, you’ll lose points.

Now, repeat after me, in your sleep:

“Exclusions: gratuitous, personal use, pre-marriage with legit heirs. Administration five years, disposition needs consent. Sale without consent is void ab initio, SPA does not cure. ART. 147 – UNIONS WITHOUT MARRIAGE (ART. 147, FC)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Art. 147 covers a man and a woman capacitated to marry each other, living exclusively together; or a void marriage. Wages and salaries are owned in equal shares. Property acquired by both through work or industry is governed by co-ownership rules. In the absence of proof to the contrary, property acquired while they lived together is presumed to have been obtained by joint efforts, work or industry, and is owned in equal shares. A homemaker is deemed to have contributed jointly. Neither party can dispose of his or her share without consent until cohabitation ends. Bad-faith party forfeits share in favor of common children, then descendants, then innocent party.

The trap: the *children* of an Art. 147 couple are *illegitimate*. The *first* ground for forfeiture is *bad-faith*; the *second* is *default*; the *third* is *waiver*.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
ART. 148 – UNIONS WITHOUT MARRIAGE (ART. 148, FC)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Art. 148 covers all other cohabitations: legal impediment, same-sex partners, married man with another woman. Wages and salaries are *not* pooled. Property is acquired only by *actual joint contribution*, in proportion to contribution (presumed equal absent proof). Homemaker has no credit. Share of a party validly married to another accrues to the ACP/CPG of the valid marriage. Forfeiture rule mirrors Art. 147.

The trap: same-sex partners are under Art. 148. The *emerging doctrine* of Josef v. Ursua. The *75/25* example: H (married to W) 75%, A 25% → A keeps 25%; H's 75% accrues to H and W's community. If A had no income, whole property belongs to H and W's community.

- 75/25 example: H married to W, A cohabiting. A keeps 25%; H’s 75% goes to H and W’s community.
- Same-sex partners: Art. 148.
- Bad-faith forfeiture: goes to common children, then descendants, then innocent party.

COMPLETE SEPARATION OF PROPERTY
--------------------------------------------------------------------

"Complete separation of property" isn’t a regime—it’s a *choice*. A written, registered settlement between spouses that says, *"What’s mine is mine, and yours is yours."* No community, no partnership, no forced sharing. The moment that settlement is signed and registered, the absolute community or conjugal partnership dissolves. No more joint administration, no more forced liquidation—just two separate lives with separate property.

The trap? It’s not automatic. You can’t just stop sharing property because you’re mad. You need that settlement, registered with the local civil registrar. And if one spouse dies? The surviving spouse’s exclusive property goes to their heirs, not the other spouse. No forced inheritance. That’s the point.

Now, the professor’s warning: *"Don’t confuse this with judicial separation."* Judicial separation is a court order, triggered by grounds like abandonment or civil interdiction. Complete separation? That’s a *contract*. One spouse can’t unilaterally declare it. Two signatures, one registration.

And here’s the kicker: if you’re Muslim, only your *first* marriage is recognized under Philippine law. That’s Tamano v. Ortiz. So if you’re a Muslim with multiple wives, only the first one’s property regime matters. The rest? Void. No community, no partnership—just separate property from the start.

---
Art. 147 vs. Art. 148: THE LINE BETWEEN LEGAL AND ILLEGAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The law draws a hard line: Art. 147 covers unions where the partners *could* marry—no legal impediment, just a choice not to. Art. 148 covers everything else: impediments, same-sex couples, bigamy, you name it.

Art. 147 is the *generous* one. Equal shares by default, even if one partner didn’t earn a peso. The homemaker gets credited. Bad faith? Forfeiture goes to the innocent party or their heirs. Art. 148? Brutal. Only what you *actually* contributed counts. No homemaker credit. Bad faith? Forfeiture goes to the *common children first*, then their descendants, then the innocent party. No spousal protection.

The professor’s red flag: *"Josef v. Ursua is coming. Memorize it."* Same-sex partners? Art. 148. Not 147. The Court said so. *"Emerging doctrine,"* she’ll quote. Don’t argue. Don’t guess. Write it down.

And here’s the exam bait: 75/25 splits. Husband contributes 75%, partner contributes 25%. Under 147? Equal shares. Under 148? 75/25. No presumption of equality. Say it with me: *"Actual contribution."*

---
THE HOMEMAKER’S SHARE: ART. 147’S SECRET WEAPON
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Under Art. 147, the homemaker isn’t left out in the cold. Their contributions—cooking, cleaning, raising kids—count as *industry*. The law presumes their share is equal unless proven otherwise. No income? No problem. The court still credits them.

But Art. 148? No such luck. If you didn’t put money or property into the union, you get *nothing*. The professor’s example: *"A stays home, B works. Under 147, A gets 50 (check: not in your notes)%. Under 148? A gets 0 (check: not in your notes)% unless they can prove they contributed something—even if it’s just babysitting so B could work."*

And the *real* trap? Bad faith. If one partner is secretly married to someone else, their share gets forfeited. Art. 147, last paragraph. Goes to the innocent spouse or their heirs. Art. 148? Forfeiture goes to the *children first*. Then descendants. Then the innocent party. No spousal favoritism.

- 75/25 Rule: Under 147, equal shares. Under 148, split by contribution.

V. THE FAMILY AND THE FAMILY HOME (ARTS. 149–162)

V. THE FAMILY AND THE FAMILY HOME (ARTS. 149–162)

--------------------------------------------------------------------

Sit. Listen. This is where the law stops being abstract and starts being *home*—and where the exam traps hide like mimics in the Underdark. The Family Code doesn’t just define who’s in your family; it defines who gets to stay in your house, who can take it, and who *can’t*—even if the deed says otherwise.

The family home isn’t just a house. It’s a shield. And like any shield, it has edges. Learn them.

---

ART. 149: THE FAMILY AS FOUNDATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The family is the foundation of the nation. No custom, practice, or agreement destructive of the family shall be recognized. That’s the opening line of Title V. Short. Absolute. The law treats family like a sacred contract—one you can’t unravel just because someone’s angry or the market crashed.

The trap? Art. 149 isn’t just moralizing. It’s a sword. If a party tries to argue that a pre-nup or a verbal agreement overrides the Family Code’s protections—*especially* for spouses or minors—you counter with this. The family home, the equal shares, the homemaker’s credit: none of it is optional. Say it like you mean it: *"This agreement is destructive of the family and shall not be recognized."*

(Like Astarion trying to weasel out of a vow. Doesn’t work. Neither does this.)

---

ART. 150: WHO’S FAMILY?
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Family relations include:
- Husband and wife.
- Parents and children (legitimate or illegitimate).
- Brothers and sisters, full or half blood.

No cousins. No in-laws. No "family friends" who helped build the house but aren’t blood. The law draws a line, and it’s not negotiable.

A problem throws in a "family dispute" between an aunt and niece sharing a house. Not family under Art. 150. Dismiss it. Fast.

---

ART. 151: SUITS BETWEEN FAMILY MEMBERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------

No suit between family members prospers unless the verified complaint alleges earnest efforts toward compromise—and proves it. No efforts? Dismissal. Automatic.

Three steps to survive this:
1. Allege the efforts in the complaint. Vague isn’t enough. *"We tried to talk"* isn’t a defense. *"We mediated on [date], offered [amount], they rejected it"* is.
2. Attach proof. A mediation certificate, a text chain, a witness. Something. Gale would call this "the Weave of evidence." Don’t let him be right about your case falling apart.
3. Know the exception. If the claim *can’t* be compromised—think child custody, support, or a void marriage—Art. 151 doesn’t apply. But if it’s a debt, a property split, or a breach of cohabitation duties? Compromise or lose.

(Like Karlach trying to split the party over rations. The axe decides. The law decides. You decide which one to cite.)

---
ART. 152–153: DEFINITION AND CONSTITUTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The family home is the dwelling house where the family resides and the land it’s on. It’s constituted jointly by:
- The spouses, *or*
- An unmarried head of the family.

Key moment: It’s constituted from the time it’s occupied as a family residence. No deed required. No formal declaration. Just: *they live there as a family, and the law sees it.*

A couple buys a vacation house but only uses it for weekends. Not a family home. The "family residence" test is primary, habitual, and continuous. Like the goblin camp—if they’re not living there full-time, it’s not home.

---
ART. 154: WHO’S PROTECTED?
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The beneficiaries aren’t just the spouses or the head. It’s everyone in the family who:
- Lives in the home, *and*
- Is dependent on the head for support.

Legitimate or illegitimate. Doesn’t matter. Parents, ascendants, descendants, brothers, sisters. Even the neighbor’s kid if they’re treated like family and rely on the head. But:
- No tenants. A boarder who pays rent? Not a beneficiary.
- No ex-spouses. Divorce severs the family tie. The home stays with the spouse who gets custody (usually), but the ex? Gone.

(Like Lae’zel’s sword forms—precise. The law is too. Don’t blur the lines.)

---
ART. 155: THE FOUR EXCEPTIONS (AND THE MORTGAGE TRAP)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The family home is exempt from execution, forced sale, or attachment—*except* for four things. Memorize them. Say them back to me.

1. Non-payment of taxes. The IRS doesn’t care if it’s your family home.
2. Debts incurred before constitution. You took out a loan to buy the land? That debt survives.
3. Debts secured by mortgages on the premises. *This is the killer.* A mortgaged family home loses its exemption. The creditor can foreclose. No mercy.
4. Debts to laborers, mechanics, builders, etc. For the construction of the building. The carpenter who built your kitchen? They get paid first.

The trap: A problem gives you a mortgaged home and asks if it’s exempt. No. It’s not. A neighbor’s family home, where they still live, can be foreclosed if it’s collateral. Say it like you mean it.

(Like Wyll’s pact—once you sign, the terms don’t change. Neither do these.)

---
ART. 156: OWNERSHIP REQUIREMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The home must be owned. Not rented. Not leased. Owned.

How?
- Part of the absolute community of property (ACP) or conjugal partnership of gains (CPG).
- The exclusive property of a spouse, with consent.
- Property of the unmarried head.

Rented? Not a family home. Grandparents’ house, but you don’t own it? Not yours. The exemption follows ownership, not occupancy.

A couple lives in their parents’ house but doesn’t own it. No family home. The parents’ home stays theirs—even if the kids are paying the rent.

(Like Scratch thinking he’s part of the party because he’s underfoot. He’s not. Neither is a squatter.)

---
ART. 157: THE PESO CEILING
--------------------------------------------------------------------

At constitution, the home’s value must not exceed:
- P300,000 in urban areas.
- P200,000 in rural areas.

Current market value doesn’t matter. If you constituted it at P300,000 and it’s now worth P2 million, the exemption only covers P300,000. A mortgage uses the current value, but again: *a mortgaged home isn’t exempt anyway.*

Why the old numbers? The Constitution set them, and Congress never updated them. Don’t argue with the exam about inflation. Just write the numbers.

(Like Astarion’s excuses for being late. They don’t change the rules.)

---
ART. 159: DURATION OF THE EXEMPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The family home survives the death of the spouses or head for:
- 10 years, *or*
- As long as there’s a minor beneficiary living there.

No partition allowed unless the court finds "compelling reasons." That’s a high bar. Assume the home stays intact.

A problem says the spouse died, but the minor child is still living there. Exemption continues. Don’t let them trick you into thinking time runs out.

(Like the tadpole in the well—small, but it lasts. So does this protection.)

---
ART. 160: CREDITOR’S REMEDY (AND THE "LOW RELEVANCE" WARNING)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

A judgment creditor not covered by Art. 155 can ask for sale of the home if it exceeds the maximum value (P300K/P200K). But the professor tagged this LOW relevance. Focus on the four exceptions and the ownership rules instead.

(Like Withers telling you to rest. Sometimes you *do* need to listen.)

---
ART. 161: ONLY ONE FAMILY HOME
--------------------------------------------------------------------

One person, one family home. You can’t constitute a second one. Even if you own three houses, only one gets the exemption.

A problem gives you a couple with two properties, both occupied as family homes. Only the first one constituted is protected. The second? Not exempt.

(Like Shar’s vows—you can’t serve two gods. Neither can you claim two family homes.)

---
ART. 162: THE FAMILY HOME IN ABSOLUTE COMMUNITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------

If the home is part of the absolute community of property, it’s jointly owned by the spouses. Death of one? The survivor gets full rights. No forced partition.

(Like Selûne’s oaths—once given, they bind. So does this.)

---
RECITATION-READY SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------

One line each. Say it in your sleep.

- Art. 149: Family is foundation; no destructive agreements.
- Art. 150: Spouses, parents/children, full/half siblings. No cousins.
- Art. 151: Earnest compromise efforts or dismissal. Exception: non-compromisable claims.
- Art. 152–153: Dwelling + land, constituted by occupation as family residence.
- Art. 154: Beneficiaries = family living there and dependent on head.
- Art. 155: Four exceptions: taxes, pre-constitution debts, mortgages, construction debts. Mortgaged home = no exemption.
- Art. 156: Must be owned (ACP/CPG/spouse’s exclusive property). Rented = nothing.
- Art. 157: P300K urban / P200K rural.
- Art. 159: 10 years or while a minor beneficiary lives there. No partition unless compelling.
- Art. 160: Creditor can force sale if home exceeds ceiling. LOW relevance.
- Art. 161: One family home per person.
- Art. 162: ACP home = survivor gets full rights.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
VI. PATERNITY AND FILIATION (ARTS. 163–182) (CONTINUED, 1 OF 6)
--------------------------------------------------------------------

We move on. You stay where you are until I say otherwise. This part covers impugning legitimacy, the action to claim filiation, and the use of surnames. No fluff.

Impugning legitimacy – the husband may attack the legitimacy of a child. Remember “may,” not “must.” The grounds are three, listed in Art. 166, FC. Say them for me.

1. Physical impossibility within the first 120 days of the 300-day period before birth – incapacity, separate residence, or serious illness.
2. Biological or scientific impossibility, except the case of artificial insemination covered in Art. 164.
3. Defective authorization in artificial insemination – mistake, fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence.

Only the husband can file, or his heirs in the three situations of Art. 171, FC. Count them.

- He died before the period expired.
- He died after filing but before a final judgment.
- The child was born after his death.

The period to file is in Art. 170, FC: 1 year if the husband (or heirs) reside where the birth was registered, 2 years elsewhere in the Philippines, 3 years abroad. The clock starts from knowledge of the birth or its registration, whichever is earlier. Registration usually precedes knowledge, so the deadline often runs from the entry in the civil register.

Trap: The biological father cannot impugn legitimacy. Cua Ko v. Republic holds the right belongs to the child, not the biological father. Memorize that.

Action to claim filiation – a child may sue for recognition of filiation for life. The action is imprescriptible for the child but extinguishes upon the child’s death. Open possession of the child’s name dies with the parent’s death; the child’s right survives.

Proof of filiation – start with the birth record. It is prima facie evidence. A handwritten instrument (affidavit, acknowledgment) must still be proven by corroborating evidence. Prima facie is enough before DNA; DNA is used to confirm the claim after the prima facie case is established (Art. 172, FC; Lucas v. Lucas).

Legitimate, illegitimate and legitimated children – Art. 36 declares a child born out of wedlock illegitimate, but the exception is that a legitimated child is considered legitimate for all intents. The “Iron Curtain Rule” from Aquino v. Aquino is gone; marital and non-marital distinctions now hinge on legitimation, not on a blanket prohibition.

Use of surnames – under Art. 176, FC and R.A. 9255, an illegitimate child takes the mother’s surname and the mother’s authority over the name. The “principally” language in Alanis v. Court of Appeals does not mean “exclusively.” The child may still bear the father’s surname if the parents agree, but the default is the mother’s.

Legitimation – occurs by subsequent marriage of the parents (Art. 164, FC). No impediment exists at conception; the child becomes legitimate from the date of marriage. The legitimation is retroactive for purposes of inheritance and civil status.

Heirs and succession – Art. 164, FC and Art. 54, FC govern the legitimation of children for succession. Legitimated children have the same rights as legitimate ones, including legitime.

Four aspects of a paternity action – Herrera v. Alba outlines: (1) filing of the complaint, (2) proof of alleged paternity, (3) rebuttal by the alleged father, (4) judgment on legitimacy or filiation.

Impugning legitimacy when the mother remarries – Arts. 168–169. If the child is born within 300 days of the former marriage, the child belongs to the former marriage if born within 180 days after the mother’s new marriage; after 180 days, the child belongs to the new marriage. Remember: LOW – “Less than One Week” after the 180-day mark flips the presumption.

Now, recite. Say “impugning legitimacy” and list the three grounds. Then say “action to claim filiation” and note it lasts for the child’s life. Finally, repeat the surname rule: mother’s surname by default, legitimation by subsequent marriage.

VI. Paternity and Filiation (Arts. 163–182) (continued, 2 of 6)

--------------------------------------------------------------------
WHO MAY IMPUGN: THE HUSBAND AND HIS HEIRS
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Listen closely, because this is where the exam tries to trick you into feeling sorry for the biological father. You will not. The law is cold, and so am I.

Only the husband may impugn the legitimacy of a child conceived or born during the marriage. And if he does not, his heirs may step in, but only in three specific cases. Count them for me. One: if the husband dies before the expiration of the period fixed for bringing his action. Two: if he dies after the filing of the complaint without having desisted from it. Three: if the child was born after the death of the husband.

That is it. Three cases. If the husband dies with a hundred billion pesos and never impugned the child, his relatives have no cause of action. The child inherits as a legitimate child. Do not let the money distract you. The right is personal to the husband, and it dies with him unless he left it in those three ways.

Now, the trap. The biological father. He cannot impugn. He cannot petition to change the certificate of live birth to put his name on it. Look at Cua Ko v. Republic, G.R. No. 210984, decided April 12, 2023. The biological father wanted his name on the certificate. The Supreme Court said no. Why? Because if his petition prospered, the child would slide from legitimate to illegitimate. That means lesser rights. The right to impugn is not the biological father’s. It belongs to the child. The child may petition on his own volition when of age. If the child changes the father, he gives up one-half of what he inherited as a legitimate child. As a lawyer, you weigh who is richer and you advise the client accordingly. But you do not invent a right for the biological father. He has none.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
PERIODS TO IMPUGN: ART. 170, FC
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The husband has three grounds to impugn, and each has a specific period. Recite them.

First ground: the husband was physically incapable of sexual intercourse with the wife at the time of conception. The period is one year from the day the child was placed under his roof.

Second ground: the husband deceived the wife. The period is one year from the day of the deception.

Third ground: the child was conceived or born during the marriage, but the husband was not the father. The period is one year from the day the child was placed under his roof, or from the day he learned of the birth if the child was born elsewhere.

Wait. Did I say one year for all of them? No. Listen again. The first ground is one year. The second ground is one year. The third ground is one year. But there is a catch. If the husband was absent from the Philippines, the period starts from the day he returned.

Now, the heirs. They have three years. Three years from the day the husband died, or from the day the child was born, whichever is later. Three cases, three years. Say it in your sleep. One year for the husband. Three years for the heirs.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
PROOF OF FILIATION: ART. 172, FC
--------------------------------------------------------------------

How does a child prove who his parents are? Illegitimate filiation is established in the same way and on the same evidence as legitimate filiation. Article 172, Family Code.

Primary proof: the record of birth in the civil register. That is the best evidence. Or a final judgment. Or an admission of filiation in a public document. The affidavit of acknowledgment of paternity at the back of the birth certificate is a public document. Or a private handwritten instrument signed by the parent.

Secondary proof, in the absence of the above: open and continuous possession of the status of a child. Photos at baptism and graduations. Christmas cards addressed to the child. Any other means allowed by the Rules of Court and special laws. That is where DNA comes in.

The trap: a handwritten instrument must still be proven. A letter attesting paternity has to be proven as such. The one who signed it must have gladly and willingly made it, and it must be his handwriting. If that is unproven, the document has no value. A layperson takes the facts and assumes them to be true. A lawyer treats nothing as a fact unless it is proven by evidence.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
DNA EVIDENCE: LUCAS V. LUCAS
--------------------------------------------------------------------

DNA is not magic. It is evidence. Look at Lucas v. Lucas. A motion for DNA testing in a filiation case requires a prima facie case or a reasonable possibility of paternity. A relationship between the mother and the putative father is enough. A DNA order is needed only if the other party refuses to give a sample. Testing can also be done extrajudicially. The rule is A.M. No. 06-11-5-SC.

Herrera v. Alba lays out four aspects of a traditional paternity action: prima facie case, affirmative defenses, presumption of legitimacy, physical resemblance. Keep those four in your head. They are your checklist.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
SURNAMES AND LEGITIMATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Legitimate children use the surname of the father. Illegitimate children use the surname of the mother, unless the mother is unknown or disclaimed. Article 176, Family Code. R.A. 9255 allows the mother to use her maiden name or the surname of the father if she chooses, but the default is the mother’s surname.

Legitimation happens by subsequent marriage. The parents must have no legal impediment to marry at the time of conception. If they could not have married at conception, the child cannot be legitimated.

The exception to the exception: Article 36. If the marriage is void due to psychological incapacity, the children born during the marriage are still legitimate. The Iron Curtain Rule is gone. Aquino v. Aquino. Marital and non-marital children are treated the same.

The action to claim filiation lasts for the life of the child. Open possession of status dies with the parent.

Use of surnames: primarily, not exclusively. Alanis v. Court of Appeals. The word “principally” does not mean “exclusively.” You can use the father’s surname primarily, but you are not forbidden from using the mother’s.

Now, quickly. One line each, and keep up.
Who may impugn: the husband, and his heirs in three cases.
Periods: one year for the husband, three years for the heirs.
Proof: birth record first, then public document, then handwritten instrument, then open possession, then DNA.
DNA: prima facie case required, Lucas v. Lucas.
Surnames: father’s for legitimate, mother’s for illegitimate, R.A. 9255.
Legitimation: subsequent marriage, no impediment at conception.
Action to claim filiation: child’s life.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
IMPUGNING LEGITIMACY
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The husband’s right to impugn legitimacy is not absolute. It’s a may, not a must. He may challenge the legitimacy of a child born within 300 days of a former marriage, but only if he acts within 120 days of learning of the child’s birth. That’s the first 120 of the 300 days. The child’s status is determined by the time of the impugnation—if the husband waits too long, the child stays legitimate.

The trap? The husband is the only one who can impugn. The child, the mother, or even the father himself cannot file this action. The biological father’s right to impugn is irrelevant—*Cua Ko v. Republic* makes that clear. The child’s right to claim filiation is separate, and it’s stronger.

The grounds are three: the child was born within 300 days of a former marriage, the child was born during a void marriage, or the child was born within 300 days of the annulment of a void marriage. Those are the only three. No others.

Now, quickly. One line each, and don’t miss a word.
- Art. 168, FC: the child born within 300 days of a former marriage.
- Art. 169, FC: the child born during a void marriage or within 300 days of its annulment.
- Art. 170, FC: the husband’s exclusive right to impugn.
- Art. 171, FC: the child’s status is fixed at the time of impugnation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
PROOF OF FILIATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The birth record is your first line of defense. If it’s there, you’re done. No further proof needed. But if the birth record is missing or contested, you move to secondary proof: a final judgment, a signed admission in a public document, or a handwritten instrument—*but* you still have to prove that handwritten thing exists and is genuine. No forgeries here.

Open and continuous possession is the last resort. It’s not enough to say, *“Well, everyone called him ‘Dad’.”* You need public, notarial, and continuous recognition—like the child using the father’s surname, the father acknowledging paternity in court, or the family treating the child as the father’s. And even then, it’s only good while the alleged parent is alive. Once they’re gone, so is your claim. *Lucas v. Lucas* makes that clear: you need a prima facie case before DNA testing becomes an option.

The trap? The child’s action to claim filiation is different. It’s not time-barred like the husband’s impugnation. The child can sue for life, and if they die young, their heirs get five years to finish the case. But open possession claims? They die with the parent.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
LEGITIMATE, ILLEGITIMATE, AND LEGITIMATED CHILDREN
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Legitimate, illegitimate, legitimated—those labels matter, but not in the way you think. The Iron Curtain Rule is gone. *Aquino v. Aquino* killed it. Non-marital children can now inherit from their legitimate relatives. The Court even changed the language: “marital” and “non-marital” instead of “legitimate” and “illegitimate.” Less stigma, same rights.

But here’s the catch: Art. 36, FC says a void marriage produces no children. None. Zero. Yet the children born from it are still legitimate. That’s the exception. The law treats them as legitimate for inheritance purposes, even if the marriage itself was invalid. Don’t let that confuse you—it’s the only time legitimacy survives a void marriage.

Now, quickly. One line each.
- Legitimate: born in a valid marriage or legitimated later.
- Illegitimate: born outside marriage, but can be legitimated by subsequent marriage of parents.
- Legitimated: same rights as legitimate children, but no retroactive effect on prior rights.
- Art. 36 exception: void marriage produces no children, *yet* the children are legitimate for inheritance.

The professor’s notes say this is medium-high priority. Don’t skip it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
ACTION TO CLAIM FILIATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

The child’s action is different from the husband’s impugnation. This one is for life. The child can sue to establish filiation—legitimate or illegitimate—anytime during their lifetime. If the child dies young, their heirs get five years to finish the case. But there’s an exception: if the claim is based on open and continuous possession, it must be brought while the alleged parent is alive. Once they’re gone, so is your claim.

Why? Because open possession isn’t written down. No birth record, no signed admission—just years of “everyone knew.” Prove it while the witnesses are still around.

The trap? The 1/2/3-year periods bind the husband. The child’s action has no time limit—except for open possession.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
USE OF SURNAMES
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Legitimate children principally use the father’s surname. That’s the rule. But “principally” doesn’t mean “exclusively.” *Alanis v. Court of Appeals* says a child raised by a single mother—where the father had no part in their life—can petition the court to use the mother’s surname instead. The Court called it “valid concern” about attributing success to someone absent.

Illegitimate children use the mother’s surname, unless the father expressly recognizes them in a birth record, public document, or handwritten instrument. Then they can use his surname. But the father can still sue to prove non-filiation during his lifetime.

The trap? R.A. No. 9255 changed the rules in 2004. Before that, acknowledged illegitimate children still bore the mother’s surname. Don’t mix up the old and new laws.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
LEGITIMATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Legitimation happens when the parents marry after the child is born. But there’s a catch: no impediment at conception. If the parents were legally barred from marrying when the child was conceived, legitimation won’t stick. That’s Art. 164, FC in action.

The child becomes legitimate from birth, with all the rights that come with it—inheritance, legitime, surname. But it doesn’t retroactively fix past rights. If the child was disinherited before legitimation, that stays.

The trap? Art. 54, FC says legitimation requires a judicial declaration if the parents are dead. No marriage after birth? No legitimation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
PATERNITY ACTIONS: THE FOUR ASPECTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------

*Herrera v. Alba* breaks paternity actions into four parts. Know them all.
1. Establishing filiation—proving who the father is.
2. Impugning legitimacy—challenging a child’s legitimacy.
3. Claiming filiation—the child’s right to establish their status.
4. Support and legitime—the financial obligations that come with filiation.

The trap? The 300-day rule applies only to impugning legitimacy. Claiming filiation has no time limit—except for open possession.

ARTICLE 164, FAMILY CODE (ARTIFICIAL INSEMINATION)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Art. 164 is the marriage’s shield. A child born during the marriage is legitimate, no questions asked—unless the marriage itself is void. But artificial insemination? That’s where the paper trail matters.

The written instrument must be signed *before* birth and recorded with the birth certificate, signed by *both* spouses. No verbal agreements, no last-minute scribbles. If the husband’s signature is forged, the child’s legitimacy dies with it. The instrument is the law’s only witness—no DNA test overrides a properly executed document. And if the sperm donor isn’t the husband? The child is still the husband’s by law, but the donor’s rights? That’s a different fight, and not one you’re having today.

The trap: Art. 164 doesn’t cover surrogacy. The mother is whoever gives birth. No contract, no arrangement—just biology. If the husband’s name isn’t on the birth certificate, the child isn’t his by law. Period.

LEGITIMATION (ARTS. 177–182)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Legitimation isn’t a favor. It’s a retroactive stamp of approval, and the law is *very* particular about who gets one.

Three requirements. One line each, and keep up.
1. The child was born *outside* wedlock—no exceptions.
2. The parents weren’t barred from marrying at conception. Bigamy? Underage? Void marriage? That’s a dealbreaker. *(R.A. 9858 covers the under-18 case, but only if the impediment is gone by marriage.)*
3. A *subsequent valid marriage* between them. No annulment later, no "oops, we forgot to get married first." The legitimation sticks, even if the marriage is voidable. *(Art. 178: annulment doesn’t un-legitimize the kids.)*

The effects? The child becomes legitimate *ab initio*—as if they’d been born in wedlock. Their legitime doubles. Their heirs inherit as legitimate heirs. And if they die before the parents marry? Their descendants get the legitimation by operation of law. *(Art. 181: the law’s way of saying "family sticks together.")*

But here’s the catch: impugnation. Only those with a direct stake can challenge it—no busybody lawsuits. And the clock starts ticking the moment they find out. Five years. *(Art. 182: shorter than Gale’s patience when Karlach eats his rations.)*

What doesn’t work?
- If the mother was still married at conception, legitimation is off the table. Adoption is the only play.
- An *Affidavit of Acknowledgment of Paternity*? That’s just the father’s name on the birth certificate. The child stays illegitimate as to the mother. *(Like Wyll’s pact—all flash, no substance.)*
- Dead parents? Too late for legitimation. Adoption or affidavit, but the child’s status doesn’t change.

The birth certificate gets an annotation: *"Legitimated by the subsequent marriage of the parents."* No fancy language, no loopholes. Just proof.

ARTICLE 166, 170, AND 171, FAMILY CODE (IMPUGNING LEGITIMACY)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Three grounds. Three chances to tear down legitimacy. And only *one* person gets to pull the trigger: the husband.

Art. 166: The child wasn’t conceived during the marriage. Simple enough. But the window is tight—only the first 120 days of the 300-day rule. *(Like the time between a mimic’s first "hello" and its first bite.)*
Art. 170: The wife was psychologically incapacitated at conception. No "I was stressed" excuses—this is *legal* incapacity. And the proof? Medical records, expert testimony. Not a guess.
Art. 171: The wife committed adultery *before* conception. The child isn’t his by blood, so legitimacy falls. But here’s the kicker: the biological father can’t impugn. This right belongs to the child. *(Cua Ko v. Republic: the law’s way of saying "some fights aren’t yours to win.")*

Who can act? Only the husband. The wife? The child? No. The law gives him the sword, and him alone. *(Like Shar’s oath—no sharing.)*

When does it expire? 300 days from the marriage’s start. After that? The child is legitimate, and the husband’s out of luck. *(Art. 168–169: the law’s way of saying "time’s up.")*

The trap: the child’s rights. Even if legitimacy is impugned, the child’s still entitled to support. The husband can’t disown them—just their legal status. *(Like Withers letting Scratch nap on the bed. Some rules are non-negotiable.)*

PROOF OF FILIATION (BIRTH RECORDS AND BEYOND)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

The birth certificate is your first line of defense. If the father’s name is on it, that’s prima facie proof—unless someone challenges it. *(Art. 172, Lucas v. Lucas: DNA doesn’t override a recorded acknowledgment.)*

But what if the record’s silent? Then you need proof:
- A handwritten instrument from the father. But it’s not enough to just *have* it—you’ve got to prove its authenticity. *(Like Gale’s "I swear I didn’t do it" face. Not good enough.)*
- Open possession. If the father treated the child as his own—support, surname, public acknowledgment—then the child’s filiation is proven. But here’s the catch: open possession dies with the parent. No father? No proof. *(Like the elder brain’s last words. Some things end.)*

The child’s right to claim filiation? It lasts a lifetime. *(Art. 175: the law’s way of saying "blood doesn’t forget.")*
The heirs’ right? Five years after the parent’s death. *(Art. 175: shorter than Karlach’s temper when hungry.)*

The trap: the biological father’s silence. If he never acknowledged the child, no amount of DNA changes that. The law protects the child’s right to know their parents, but it won’t force a man to claim them. *(Like Wyll’s pact—sometimes, the deal’s already made.)*

---------------------------------------------------------------------
USE OF SURNAMES (ART. 176, ALANIS V. COURT OF APPEALS)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Legitimate children take the father’s surname. Illegitimate? The mother’s, unless the father steps up.

Art. 176: The father’s recognition can be:
- A birth certificate with his name.
- A public document (notarized affidavit, court order).
- A private handwritten instrument (but again—proof of authenticity is required).

But here’s the kicker: "principally" doesn’t mean "exclusively." *(Alanis v. CA: the law’s way of saying "you can add a middle name, but don’t get cute.")*
- Legitimate: father’s surname *principally*, but the mother’s can be added.
- Illegitimate: mother’s surname *principally*, but the father’s can be added if recognized.

R.A. 9255 tweaked this for illegitimate children: if the father’s recognized, the child can use *both* surnames—mother’s first, father’s second. *(Like the goblin camp’s "take what you want" rule. Mostly.)*

The trap: no joint custody. Even if the father’s on the birth certificate, he doesn’t get parental authority. That’s the mother’s alone. *(Like Astarion’s "I’ll handle it" promises. You know better.)*

---------------------------------------------------------------------
LEGITIMATE VS. LEGITIMATED VS. ILLEGITIMATE CHILDREN
---------------------------------------------------------------------

Three categories. Three sets of rights. And one big exception.

Legitimate: Born in wedlock. Full rights, full inheritance.
Legitimated: Born out of wedlock, but parents later married. Same rights as legitimate, retroactive.
Illegitimate: Born outside wedlock, no legitimation. Half the legitime, mother’s surname by default.

The exception? A void marriage produces legitimate children. *(Aquino v. Aquino: the Iron Curtain Rule is dead, but the exception lives.)* No annulment, no challenge—just legitimacy by default.

The trap: legitimated ≠ legitimate by blood. They’re in the same category for rights, but the law treats them as one group. No splitting hairs. *(Like Lae’zel’s sword forms—pick one, and stick with it.)*

ACTION TO CLAIM FILIATION (ART. 175)

The child’s right to know their parents doesn’t expire. For life. *(Art. 175: the law’s "never give up" rule.)*

WHO MAY BE ADOPTED
--------------------------------------------------------------------

DOMESTIC ADMINISTRATIVE ADOPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

This isn’t in the Family Code. It’s R.A. No. 11642, Title III, Chapter 3, and the NACC handles it. Two routes: domestic administrative adoption (for relatives within the 4th civil degree) and intercountry adoption (for non-relatives or relatives beyond the 4th degree). The NACC’s role is to screen, approve, and supervise—not to judge. If the NACC says no, you appeal to the Court of Appeals in 10 days. That’s the CDCLAA route. Memorize it.

The professor’s red flag: *"No CDCLAA for relatives within the 4th degree."* If the adoptee is your cousin’s child, you’re out of luck. The NACC’s decision is final. For everyone else? Appeal or bust.

EFFECTS OF ADOPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Adoption doesn’t just change names. It rewrites the family tree. The adoptee becomes the adopter’s legitimate child, with all the rights and obligations that come with it. New birth certificate, new inheritance line, new everything. The biological parents’ rights vanish—unless the adoptee is a relative within the 4th degree, and the adoption is domestic administrative. Then, the biological parents keep some rights, but the adopter gets parental authority.

Reciprocal succession? Yes. The adoptee inherits from the adopter, and vice versa. Like they were born to each other. The professor’s note: *"No half-measures."* Either it’s full legitimacy, or it’s nothing.

The trap? The adoptee’s existing children. If the adopter already has kids, adoption doesn’t dilute their inheritance unless the adoptee is under 18 and unmarried. Then, the adoptee’s share is carved out of the adopter’s estate. Think of it like adding a new branch to the family tree—it grows from the same trunk, but it doesn’t uproot the old branches.

The professor’s warning: *"No second chances."* If the adoptee waits too long, the adoption stands. No appeals, no exceptions.

EFFECTS OF ADOPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Adoption doesn’t just change names—it rewrites the family tree. Here’s what happens:

1. Legitimacy. The adoptee becomes a legitimate child of the adopter, with all the rights and duties that come with it. No more "illegitimate" status. The adopter’s spouse must adopt the child too—unless the spouse is already dead or legally separated. (Yes, Lae’zel would argue that’s a loophole. No, it’s not.)

2. New birth certificate. The Civil Registrar General issues an amended certificate of live birth, cutting all ties to the biological family. No more "foundling" or "unknown father" entries. If the adoptee is over 18, they can still get the change—but they’ll need a court order first.

3. Reciprocal succession. The adoptee inherits from the adopter, and vice versa. They’re family now. But—and this is critical—the adoptee does not inherit from the biological family unless the adoption is rescinded. That’s the trade-off.

The adoptee also loses rights to the biological family’s estate, unless the adoption is rescinded. No double-dipping.

One more thing: Adoption doesn’t erase criminal liability. If the adoptee commits a crime, the adopter can’t just disown them to avoid prosecution. The law doesn’t work that way.

RESCISSION OF ADOPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Only the adoptee can rescind the adoption. Not the adopter. Not the biological parents. Only the adoptee. If the adopter wants out, their remedy is disinheritance—not rescission. (Astarion would try to weasel his way around this. Don’t let him.)

Grounds (and they’re brutal):
1. The adopter repeatedly physically or verbally abuses the adoptee, even after counseling.
2. The adopter attempts to kill the adoptee.
3. The adopter abandons the adoptee and fails to meet parental duties.

If any of these happen, the adoptee can petition the NACC to rescind. The NACC will restore their custody if they’re still a minor, cancel the amended birth certificate, and reinstate the original one. The adoptee’s rights to the adopter’s estate revert—but only from the date of approval. Any vested rights (like an inheritance already received) stay put.

Criminal penalties? Still apply. Abuse, attempted murder, abandonment—those are crimes, and the law doesn’t forget that.

Minor adoptees? The social welfare officer helps them file. If they’re over 18 but incapacitated, their guardian steps in.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
RESCISSION VS. DISINHERITANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------

This is where students trip up. Rescission undoes the adoption entirely. Disinheritance just cuts off inheritance rights—without ending the legal relationship.

- Rescission: Only the adoptee can do this. It wipes the adoption clean.
- Disinheritance: The adopter does this. The adoptee stays adopted, but they lose inheritance rights.

If the adopter is the one being abused, they can’t rescind the adoption. Their only option is to disinherit the adoptee—and hope the adoptee doesn’t retaliate. (Karlach would understand that logic. The rest of us? Less so.)

- Effects: legitimacy, new birth certificate, reciprocal succession—but no inheritance from biological family unless rescinded.
- Rescission: only the adoptee can do it, on three brutal grounds. Effects include restoring custody, canceling the amended birth certificate, and reverting succession rights (from approval date onward).
- Disinheritance ≠ rescission. One ends the adoption; the other just cuts off inheritance.

WHO MAY ADOPT
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Twenty-five. That’s the minimum age to adopt. Twenty-five, or the court won’t even look at your petition. And you’d better be sixteen years older than the child—no exceptions. The law doesn’t care if you’re “emotionally mature” for a twelve-year-old. The numbers are the numbers.

Five years’ residency is next. You’ve got to be a *permanent* part of the child’s life, not just a vacation fling. And if you’re married? Your spouse’s consent is required—unless they’re dead, missing, or legally separated. No loopholes.

The trap? Stepparents get special treatment. If you’re married to the child’s parent, you can adopt without the sixteen-year rule. But the twenty-five and five years still apply. Don’t assume you’re exempt.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
CONSENT TO ADOPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Ten. That’s when the child gets a say. Ten years old, and their consent is required. Below that? The court decides. But if they’re over ten, you’d better have their signature—or you’re wasting your time.

Parents? Their consent is *always* required, unless they’re dead, missing, or their rights have been terminated. And if the child is over eighteen? Too late. Adoption is off the table.

The trap? Foster parents can consent for abandoned children. But only if the biological parents are *truly* gone. The court won’t let you adopt just because you’ve got a soft spot for a kid in your care.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
WHO MAY BE ADOPTED
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Three years. That’s how long an adult must be treated as your own child before the court will recognize the adoption. Three years of acting like family—no paperwork, no legalities, just *living* as one. After that, you can petition for formal adoption.

But if the parents are dead? The clock drops to six months. The law gives you a break when there’s no one left to object.

The trap? The child must be under eighteen. If they’re older, you’re out of luck. No exceptions. The court won’t let you adopt a twenty-year-old just because you’ve been raising them since they were five.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
CDCLAA: ABANDONMENT AND PROCEDURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Three months. That’s how long a child must be abandoned before the court can declare them legally free. Three months of no contact, no support, no care—and then the state steps in.

But the clock starts *after* you’ve tried to find them. Five days of posting notices in the local paper and at the courthouse. Then fifteen working days of waiting for objections. If no one claims them? The court moves forward.

The trap? The child must be under seventeen. If they’re older, the rules change. And if someone *does* object? You’ve got ten days to appeal the decision. Don’t miss it.

And don’t forget: seven working days to serve the notice on the last known guardian. The law doesn’t bend for hurry.
